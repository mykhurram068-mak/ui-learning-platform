4 stages · ~6–8 hours · Foundation

Make the app know who's using it.
Users. Passwords. Sessions. Roles.

Every production app has to answer two questions on every request: who is this? (authentication) and what can they do? (authorization). This course teaches both, in Flask, from the ground up. A users table. A password hash that can't be reversed. A session cookie that survives a reload. A role that changes what a user can see. Then, in the final stage, we drop the whole pattern into the HR & Attendance build — the cleanest two-audience app in the studio — and watch the manager/employee split become enforced by the database instead of the honor system.

~6–8 hours Intermediate Python · Flask · werkzeug · SQLite 4 stages · foundation course

What this course is: the foundation of authentication. A users table with email and password. Hashing, correctly. A session cookie and a @login_required decorator. A role column and a @role_required decorator. Then applied to the HR build so you see it work in a real app, not a toy demo.

What this course is not: OAuth, MFA, password reset, email verification, rate limiting. Each is a course of its own. This course teaches the foundation those other courses assume.

Why this course exists

The gap between "works on my laptop" and "could run for a real user".

Every build in the studio — CRM, Inventory, POS, HR, Delivery — has the same missing piece. There's no way to tell who's using the app. The CRM has a basic username field but no password. The others have no user concept at all. That's fine for a teaching build; it's wrong for anything that ships.

Authentication is the smallest change that closes the biggest gap. It's six things: a users table, a register form, a login form, a logout route, a password hash, a session cookie. That's it. Once you have those, the app knows who's asking. Once you add roles, it knows what they can do.

The reason this is a course and not a build stage: it's a pattern, not a domain. Auth doesn't care whether the app is a CRM or an HR system. The users table is the same. The hash is the same. The session is the same. The role check is the same. So it belongs in a course, where you learn it once and apply it five times.

What you'll learn

Seven concepts, each with a small code surface and a large conceptual payload.

Hashing vs encryption

One-way vs two-way. Why passwords must be hashed, never encrypted. Why "we'll encrypt the passwords" is a red flag in a vendor conversation.

Salts

Why the same password produces different hashes for different users. Why a rainbow table stops working. What werkzeug does automatically that you'd otherwise do manually.

Session cookies

What's in the cookie (a random ID). What's on the server (the actual user data). Why you can't forge a session. Why logging out is deleting the session row, not clearing the cookie.

Authentication vs authorization

"Who are you?" vs "what can you do?". One happens at login, the other on every request. Why confusing the two is a common source of security bugs.

Decorators

@login_required and @role_required are functions that wrap functions. Understanding them is understanding the pattern: "before this route runs, check something."

Role-based access control

The simplest form of authorization. A role column. A check per route. Viewer, editor, admin. Why this is enough for most apps and what patterns replace it at scale.

Sessions vs JWT

When each is correct. Server-rendered HTML uses sessions. Stateless APIs (mobile, microservices) use JWT. Neither is "better" — they solve different problems.

Applying it to a real app

The most important lesson: none of this matters until it's in a real app. Stage 04 shows the pattern carried into the HR build, and the change is small and visible.

Why this course is text-heavy and code-light

The payload is the pattern, not the typing.

Every stage in this course has 4–6 code snippets of 10–20 lines each. That's the minimum that makes the point. The rest is explanation: why this approach, what alternative would also work, what happens if you skip it, what the failure mode looks like.

This is deliberate. Auth is not a typing exercise. The code is small. What makes it hard is knowing what to do, why, and what to never do. A tutorial that gives you 500 lines of a working auth system teaches you how to copy 500 lines. A course that gives you 60 lines and 3,000 words of explanation teaches you how to think about auth.

The full files (delivered separately) are small — a models.py, an auth.py, maybe a template each for login and register. You can paste them into any Flask app. The lesson pages tell you which lines matter and why. The full files are the answer; the lesson pages are the reasoning.

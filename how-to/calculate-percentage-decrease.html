📉 How to Calculate Percentage Decrease

Updated October 2026 · 5 min read

To calculate percentage decrease, subtract the new value from the original, divide by the original, then multiply by 100. For example, going from 80 to 60 is a 25% decrease because ((80 − 60) ÷ 80) × 100 = 25.

This guide walks through the formula with three worked examples, covers the difference between decrease and change, and links to a free calculator so you can skip the manual math.

The Percentage Decrease Formula

The Formula
% Decrease = ((Original − New) ÷ Original) × 100

Three steps:

  1. Subtract: Original value − New value = the drop.
  2. Divide: Drop ÷ Original value = the decimal change.
  3. Multiply: Decimal change × 100 = the percentage.

The result is always a positive number — even though the value went down. This is different from percentage change, which uses a negative sign to indicate a decrease.

Example 1: A Price Cut

A jacket was $120 and is now on sale for $84. What's the percentage decrease?

Worked Example
Step 1: 120 − 84 = 36
Step 2: 36 ÷ 120 = 0.30
Step 3: 0.30 × 100 = 30%
→ 30% off — the jacket is 30% cheaper

Example 2: Falling Website Traffic

Your site had 12,000 visits last month and 9,600 this month. What's the drop?

Worked Example
Step 1: 12,000 − 9,600 = 2,400
Step 2: 2,400 ÷ 12,000 = 0.20
Step 3: 0.20 × 100 = 20%
→ 20% decrease in traffic

Example 3: Stock Price Drop

A stock fell from $45.50 to $36.40. What percentage did it lose?

Worked Example
Step 1: 45.50 − 36.40 = 9.10
Step 2: 9.10 ÷ 45.50 = 0.20
Step 3: 0.20 × 100 = 20%
→ The stock dropped 20%

Percentage Decrease vs. Percentage Change

These two terms are often confused. Here's the difference:

  • Percentage change uses the same formula but produces a signed result: +30% for an increase, −30% for a decrease. It's a general-purpose term.
  • Percentage decrease always produces a positive number. You mention "decrease" to signal the direction.

So "20% decrease" and "−20% change" mean the same thing, but you'd never say "−20% decrease" — that's a double negative.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Dividing by the new value instead of the original. Always use the original as the base. This is the #1 mistake in percentage decrease problems.
  • Reported as negative when it should be positive. If you say "a −25% decrease," that actually means a 25% increase. Use positive numbers for decreases.
  • Confusing the discount with the final price. "30% off" means the new price is 70% of the original, not 30% of it.
  • Assuming decreases are symmetric with increases. A 50% decrease requires a 100% increase to get back to the start. A 20% decrease requires a 25% increase to recover.

Recovery math: If a stock drops 50%, it must gain 100% just to break even. This is why recovering from losses is harder than the original drop suggests — and why investors pay close attention to percentage decrease.

How to Find the Original Price (Reverse Percentage)

Shopping sales often hide the original price. Here's how to work backward:

Reverse Formula
Original = Final ÷ (1 − Decrease % ÷ 100)
Example — Finding the Pre-Sale Price
Final price: $75, Discount: 25%
Step 1: 1 − 0.25 = 0.75
Step 2: 75 ÷ 0.75 = 100
→ The original price was $100

Frequently Asked Questions

Can percentage decrease be more than 100%?

No. A 100% decrease means the value dropped to zero. A decrease above 100% would require the value to go negative, which isn't meaningful for prices, populations, or most real-world numbers.

How do I calculate a discount percentage?

Discount % = ((Original Price − Sale Price) ÷ Original Price) × 100. Example: $80 → $60 is ((80 − 60) ÷ 80) × 100 = 25% off.

What's the difference between percentage decrease and percentage point decrease?

Percentage decrease measures relative change. A percentage point decrease measures absolute change between two percentages. If a rate drops from 20% to 15%, that's a 5 percentage point drop — but a 25% relative decrease.

How do I calculate average percentage decrease over time?

Use the CAGR formula: ((End ÷ Start)^(1/n) − 1) × 100, where n is the number of periods. Never average percentages directly — that gives the wrong answer.

What does a 0% decrease mean?

It means the value didn't change. The original and new values are identical.
